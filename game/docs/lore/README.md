## Sense check

Lore makes sense to the people in it. Ask every practice, deal and detail these five questions from inside the world, with only what its people know and want. That covers lore pages, story game output, quest lines, talk, voice sheets, item lore and term tips.

1. Purpose: what is it for, in one plain sentence, as the people who do it see it?
2. It works: does it do that job in this world, or would its people believe it does? Does any other fact stop it, such as the dry climate, what is scarce or what the basin can make?
3. Everyone has a reason: why would each other person or group it names bother, by what the lore already says they want?
4. It earns its place: does it change a choice, a scene or a piece of evidence the player can find, or is it there only to look woven or exact?
5. The game already says why: when a line explains something the game does, like what a town buys, makes or needs, does it give the reason the game's rules give? Find that rule in `src/data/` first, and never invent a second cause.

A missing answer, a clash with another fact or an answer that would make a basin driver laugh cuts or fixes the detail. Keep the parts around it that pass. These patterns fail most often:

- Self-defeating practice: a group does what undoes its own purpose. Beacon keepers who put their lamps out at night fail it.
- Means that don't fit the goal: folk means aimed at something huge, unless the lore means it as a joke. Oil lamps to signal a starship fail it.
- Name-drop garnish: a faction or a named person tied to a detail with no motive, so the lore looks connected. A Pierce lien on a cult's lamps fails it.
- Precision garnish: exact counts, ages and spans that no scene or evidence uses. Six lamps kept for sixty years fail it.
- Invented cause: a reason made up for a game rule that already has one, which then clashes with the world. Bowl buying scrap because the rains rust its sluice hinges every year fails it twice: rain is rare in the basin, and Bowl buys scrap because its machinists turn it into parts.
- A thing the world doesn't have: a vehicle, machine or trade the game never shows. A water tanker fails it, since water travels in barrels on ordinary trucks.

### Cut examples

Each of these was in the lore and was cut or fixed. The test it failed is in brackets.

- The Granary paid Kiln a sack for every armed truck that crossed. [It works] Kiln strips trucks that neither pay nor fight, so an armed truck is the one that needs the deal least. A racket charges for passage. Now Kiln eats Granary rations, and Granary trucks pass.
- Tomas was sentenced to ten years in the Bowl patrol for scraping chalk off a meter. [It works] Ten years in the patrol make a tenant a citizen with a vote, so the sentence was a reward. Now he simply serves in the patrol.
- Jory wanted a vote for every tenant who serves ten years, which the rule already gave, and Dag said tenants in the patrol stay off the citizens' roll. [It works] Two facts that cannot both hold. Now tenants have no vote, ten years in the patrol put a tenant on the roll, and citizens vote.
- Sefa never took the last water from anyone. [It earns its place] A quirk with no scene behind it. It leaked into the game as a question a player called nonsense.
- Aurel Brede, a household steward, signed patrol postings, and Ilya's postings came from him. [Everyone has a reason] Nothing gives a steward power over the patrol. The link was there only to tie two people together.
- Lena Graves woke in a pod as a girl in year 0 and still warned command in year 130, and Dagny Brask still worked her furnace past 110. [It works] Nobody in the basin lives to 140 without the Apprentice's implants. Now Lena Graves is dead and the Nose mechanic is another Lena, and Dagny is past 70.
- The Salvage Yard bored the second pin into Nose swivels. [Purpose, It earns its place] No one said what the pin was for, and nothing used it.
- The Guild's trust owned Old Orchard's machine tools and rented them to Nose. [Everyone has a reason] A message guild has no reason to own an army's workshop. Now Nose hauled the tools away once the army was gone.
- Sabra Kell gave toll receipts honored once a year, and left her tin by Ladle's scraper. [Purpose, It earns its place] A receipt honored once a year does no job, and the tin and scraper were props with no meaning.
- Ilya Moreno feared a truck going missing on his watch and repainted the same count every morning, though he let the two staff cars out himself. [It works] His fear contradicted his own secret, and the daily habit changed nothing.
- Odile Marsh counted every sack at night and chalked the count on the Granary gate, and the Bowl patrol was paid from the Granary count. [Purpose, It works] Nobody in the basin counts as a job, and a count the steward chalks herself is still only her word. Now Odile keeps the gate open by day, the Kiln ration crates stand stacked beside it, and the patrol is paid from the Granary's grain.
- Half the basin counted as its practice: Agnes read meters into a pump book, Mirela counted barrels into a Canal book, Ines read the sky count aloud, Petra wrote down every truck with the hour, Hollis chalked drum counts, the President read the ration tally, and clues hung on tables of numbers. [Purpose, It earns its place] People who scrape by on water and grain do not keep books of figures, and a number on a page shows the player nothing. Now each person does a plain thing that fits their wants, and every clue is something the player sees or hears, like a loaded truck, an empty bay or a tone matched to iron bars.

## Icarus

Icarus was a fertile farming basin before the collapse. Orchards, fields, canals, irrigation gates, farm roads, and market towns shaped the land. It was not a spaceflight center. The name Icarus came after the crash. Its old name has not been chosen.

A huge spaceship broke apart while descending through the atmosphere. Its largest hull section lies in the central crater and is called the Fallen Sun. Engines, cargo sections, and escape pods fell across the basin, leaving a trail of wrecks and impact scars. No one in the basin agrees whether the crash happened before, during, or after the wider collapse. It happened in the middle of the Chaos, as [World facts](world.md) records.

People still live and trade here. Farmers protect the canals that still work and the remaining seed stores. Salvagers recover metal and machinery from the wrecks. Truckers carry food, water, fuel, and parts between settlements. An irrigation gate can matter as much as a cargo hold.

The landscape shows all three histories: old field boundaries and canals, the ship's debris trail, and newer truck roads connecting useful places. Hills, valleys, a canyon, and a dried riverbed divide the routes. A rim of hills closes the basin, and a mountain range rises behind Nose in the north-east. The old farm belt lies along the dried river in the west, from Old Orchard to Bowl, and only Bowl's stretch of its canals still holds water. Since the river sank, sand has drifted south-east on the wind into a desert of dunes, where the Glass Flats lie. In the low north-west the groundwater that feeds Dustwell seeps up into a swamp of black mud. Its water is foul, so nobody barrels it. The crash's date and the basin's old name remain open.

After the Chaos, Nose and Bowl fought a war over the basin. The Old Army ended it and kept the peace between them, until it ran short of food and left Old Orchard. The towns have not fought openly since. Each one expects the other to start.

### Open questions

Each of these needs one true answer, written here once it is chosen. The game will show it only as evidence.

- Why the ship came back and why it fell. It was an Exodus ship, as [Timeline](timeline.md) records.
- Whether Nose's wreck is part of the Fallen Sun's ship or a second ship.
- What lies in the Fallen Sun's crater. What the green glow does to people is answered: it is the ship's leaking reactor core, and its sickness brings the fever dreams.
- Why the Old Army left Old Orchard.
- What the Given were. Many basin people descend from these pod survivors, as [Peoples](peoples.md) records. Whether they were sleeping passengers or people the ship grew, and why they woke remembering nothing, is open.
- What the basin was called before.

Chosen answers live in the hidden truths of [Timeline](timeline.md). The fused glass of the Glass Flats is answered there.

### Mood references

- Huge Old World structures turned into homes: a cruise liner, an aircraft carrier, the oil platforms of Neft Daşları on the Caspian Sea.
- Ernst Haeckel's Art Forms in Nature, for beauty found in strange shapes.

## Item lore

Every part, good and the parcel has a lore entry in `src/text/en/lore.ts` and `src/text/ru/lore.ts`. It shows under the name in the item's hover tip and as the last paragraph of the inspection card and the shop row's open detail. It is in-world common knowledge, like a term tip: one or two plain, dry sentences on what the thing is in the basin, who makes or uses it, why drivers pick it and what is odd about it.

Writing rules:

- No game rule, stat, tier or number, and no digits. Never restate what the card shows: mass, range, arc, damage, footprint or condition. A claim about how an item behaves is road wisdom that matches a real rule in the [mechanics pages](wiki/mechanics/).
- Every fact matches this lore. It is evidence, never a conclusion: no hidden truth from [Timeline](lore/timeline.md), no ship name, no use for the M's, no crash date and no old name of the basin. No faction is right.
- Name only Bowl, Nose and groups any driver hears of on day one: the Old Army, the Nose Army, the Bowl Farmers, raiders and Pierce. Name no other site and no person.
- Each entry has its own image of New World use of Old World leftovers where it fits. No two entries share a template sentence.
- Read the entry against the [Humanizer](../writing/humanizer.md#how-roam-uses-it) the way its How ROAM uses it section says for fiction.
- At most 200 characters in English and 270 in Russian. `src/text/catalog.test.ts` checks digits, length, the out-of-character words and repeats.

Bad: "A machine gun turret with a wide arc that fires six rounds." It states stats. Good: "The workshop line at Nose turns these out by the crate. Nobody calls it a good gun, but it fires when it is dirty, and every welder in the basin can fix it."

## M's

The currency of the basin is the M, and many are M's. An M is a smooth, indestructible metal disc inscribed with an M. M's were used for something aboard the colony ship before it crashed. The inscribed M is the ship's logo, because the ship's real name began with M. Drivers do not know this. To them an M is simply what things cost.

The Fallen Sun is the basin's name for the ship's largest hull section. It is not the ship's name.

Writing rules:

- In game text say "M's" for an amount and "M" for one. Never call this currency "money", "coin", "cash" or "credits", in labels, tooltips or lines. The code keeps `money` as its internal name.
- An M is a metal coin. There is no paper money: never write notes, bills, «купюры» or «бумажки».
- In Russian say «М» for any amount, like «50 М» and «Не хватает М». Never «деньги» or «монеты».
- A label never names the currency next to the M icon. The icon says it.
- Never name the ship, and never say what the discs were used for. Both are still open.
- Show an amount as a number with the coin glyph in panels, and as "N M's" in the log, dialogue and errors. `src/ui/money.ts` owns both.

## Waste Of Time Radio

Jill Jane, "J.J.", runs Waste Of Time Radio from a mast nobody has found. Her broadcasts stream onto the pager screen of the truck's radio. `src/data/radio.ts` holds her lines. Keep new lines in this voice:

- She reads the wasteland like an Old World farm-and-weather report, with crop-report formality, though no crops are left. That is the New World against the Old World.
- She is dry and gallows-funny. She calls listeners "road machiners" or "boys" and signs off with "Waste some time."
- She reports raids as business and never judges how anyone lives. She does frown on killing.
- She stays in character. She never says turns, quests, XP, levels, HP, keys, clicks, the player or the game. She never states a game stat as a number, like damage, HP or a rule value. Ordinary numbers people say are fine.
- Hints are road wisdom someone in the wasteland would say, like "Noon sun cooks an engine. Park in shade." Each matches a real rule in the [mechanics pages](../wiki/mechanics/). A hint never says what to do next or how a control works.
- She names only places the listener has found. Anywhere else is a basin direction like "up north" or "in the southwest basin". She never quotes prices. The Rumor mill and Market ears perks keep that work.
- A line fits the three-line screen: 90 characters at most once its names are filled in.
- Read each line against the [Humanizer](../writing/humanizer.md#how-roam-uses-it) the way its How ROAM uses it section says for fiction.

## Settlement people

Bowl and Nose have locals the player talks to in town. `src/data/locals.ts` holds them and the journal notes their rumors leave. What each says is an ink quest in `src/data/quests/`. Keep new lines in these voices:

- Bowl is run by the canal families, a say for every gate, and guarded by the Bowl Farmers. Its people talk warm and slow, of crops, canals, water and seed. They think Nose is soldiers playing at a town.
- Nose is an Army post that became a town. The Army runs it: command gives orders, dispatch hands them out. Its people talk short and to the point. They think Bowl is slow but needed, since its grain feeds them.
- Bowl's grain is sorgut, a hardy sorghum the canal families grow on water trucked in from Dustwell, or from Green Pit when the Dustwell road is bad. The Granary cooks it with salt and water into rations, the tins every truck eats on the road, and sells them back to Bowl, Nose and the stalls.
- The locals are Ruben the canal warden, Hattie of the gate yard water stand and Dag of the Farmers patrol at Bowl, and Sergeant Kovac at dispatch, Lena the mechanic and Old Ibo the radio man at Nose. Nose Army wagon Seven, which ran Dustwell water to Bowl and Granary rations back to Nose, went dark on the road, and its crew walked home.
- Talk about the recent years comes from more locals. Jory Baptiste and Bo Lindqvist are at Bowl, a Bowl patrol driver is at the Granary, Sefa Krol trades at the Salvage Yard, and Ines Abend and an Order Bureau clerk are at Nose.
- Jory speaks for the tenants and ends every answer with a short joke. His subjects are the vote, the patrol, Kiln and the grain, and the household truck to the top terraces.
- Bo is gentle and plain. He tells the fight at the Burnt Convoy once and does not excuse it.
- The patrol driver talks like a working Bowl hand: the Kiln crates, the road no one guards and the north-western tale. The Bureau clerk talks short and low, afraid of the corridor, and says only what the corridor says.
- Sefa is blunt and dry. She speaks for the families off the Burnt Convoy, who live at the Salvage Yard now. She takes what the road is done with and leaves the water.
- Ines is level and open. She rings the plate's bars against the radio tone for anyone who stops and says plainly what she does not know.
- The six older locals also talk about Pierce's gun rule, the swivel, brake rods, Pierce paper, the Kiln fee, the tenants, Green Pit and the Glass Flats, each in their own voice. Ruben, Hattie and Dag disagree with Kovac, Lena and Ibo on who is to blame.
- A rumor from [Rumors](rumors.md) reaches the journal as a note that records what the speaker said, never the truth. A biased speaker says so in character, and two locals may contradict each other, as Sefa and Ibo do on the old Burnt Convoy stretch.
- Locals point to the people of other jobs by name and place only: Agnes Ondra at Dustwell, Odile Marsh at the Granary, Mara Kessel at the Salvage Yard, Constance Pierce at the Pierce office in Nose, Tamsin Roe the courier, Lusa Fenwick and Pim Arslan the lens cutter. They never speak for them.
- Read each line against the [Humanizer](../writing/humanizer.md#how-roam-uses-it) the way its How ROAM uses it section says for fiction.
- Locals may be wrong, biased or out of date. Two of them may contradict each other. No line settles the crash date, the basin's old name or what the Fallen Sun was.
- Lines stay in character, as J.J.'s do. They never say turns, quests, XP, levels, HP, markers, waypoints, the map, clicks, the player or the game. They never state a game stat as a number, like damage, HP or a rule value. Ordinary numbers people say are fine, like "a decade of service" or "two cans".
- A clue describes land the listener can see: a compass direction from a town, a named road or place, and the terrain there, like a hollow or an old farm with a water tower. It may give a rough spoken distance, like "half a day north", but never a map unit or an exact spot.
