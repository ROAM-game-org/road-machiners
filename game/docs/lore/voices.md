# Voices

Each speaker has a voice sheet. Write a line in the voice, then hide the name and read it again. If another person in the basin could say it, rewrite it. The rules for speech are in [Writing quests](../quest-authoring.md#writing-rules).

## Nose, the Salvage Yard and the road

### Sergeant Kovac

Vocabulary: orders, the sheet, the log, the roster, schedules, fuel counts and hours. He compares things to duty rosters and rations.

Rhythm: clipped military sentences, one fact and then what follows from it. He uses contractions the way a soldier does, like "Don't be late" and "I won't reopen it".

Habits: he says "I log it" and "That is above my sheet". He asks a stranger to state business and keep it short.

Cares about: order, his fuel count and the soldiers on his roster. He avoids any opinion about command, and he goes quiet about Emil Kessel.

Strangers: polite and wary. He tells a stranger exactly what he can give and what he cannot.

Sample: "Nose. The dispatch desk is mine. Freight on the north-east road or through the canyon crossing passes this desk, or it doesn't pass."

### Lena

Vocabulary: bench, weld, bolt, pin, plate, load, grease and bearings. She compares everything to parts that hold or fail.

Rhythm: quick and bossy. She gives an order, then the reason, then a verdict of one short sentence. She uses many contractions and talks loosely.

Habits: she says "Put it on the bench" and "Show me". She ends advice with a plain order, like "Pay it."

Cares about: trucks that bring their drivers home, and welds a person can read. She avoids politics, and she grumbles that nobody asks a mechanic.

Strangers: she looks at the truck before she looks at the face. She talks to a stranger as to a customer who may be about to make a mistake.

Sample: "Every part does one job and fails at another. Heavy plate stops rounds, but it eats your speed. A big gun eats your deck on top of that. So buy what the road needs, not what shines on the shelf."

### Old Ibo

Vocabulary: the air, static, bands, calls, signals, cold nights and weather. He compares people and rumors to signals that fade, drift and cross.

Rhythm: long, wandering sentences with an aside in the middle. He circles back and finishes with a dry joke. He uses contractions and rambles.

Habits: he starts with "Eh?" and says "I would wager". He says "I heard it on the air" and "Do not tell the Sergeant".

Cares about: the air, the old days and company on a cold night. He avoids saying who tells him things, and he mocks any man who is sure of a rumor.

Strangers: he offers a crate to sit on and wants the news the stranger carries.

Sample: "Nobody knows, but I'd wager she moves it. Her signal's strongest at night, and it drifts like the mast is on wheels."

### Ines Abend

Vocabulary: the count, the number, the tower, the year, the sky and the plate. She compares things to tallies and to reading.

Rhythm: level and gentle. Her sentences are complete and unhurried, and she often ends a thought with one short sentence that sums it up.

Habits: she says "I do not know" plainly, and she says what she believes apart from what she knows. She offers to read the number before anyone asks.

Cares about: the number being open to everyone. She avoids guessing what happens when the count stops, and she does not blame the Sunward.

Strangers: she gives a stranger the number first and her name second.

Sample: "A number kept in one head is a number kept from everyone. We voted on it, and I spoke first."

### The Bureau clerk

Vocabulary: files, drawers, boxes, signatures, copies and the corridor. He compares people to papers.

Rhythm: low and careful. His sentences are soft and passive, and he stops short of names.

Habits: he says "the corridor says" before any claim. He says "I do not open it" and "for the file". He checks the door.

Cares about: not being named in a file himself. He avoids saying anything as his own opinion.

Strangers: suspicious. He asks a stranger to keep a voice down before he answers anything.

Sample: "I only say what the corridor says. A clerk at the Pierce office sold the roads of insured trucks to robbers. When it came out, he was sent west and told to stay."

### Sefa Krol

Vocabulary: wrecks, rims, wiring, rust, beans, water and her mother. She compares people to what the road leaves behind.

Rhythm: dry and blunt. She says a flat fact, then a sharper sentence, then a joke about her mother or the beans. She asks a question and answers it herself.

Habits: she says "That is all we are". She prices what a stranger carries. She says a good deal is one nobody likes.

Cares about: the families, fair trade and leaving a driver his last water. She avoids names of people who came to the convoy for shelter.

Strangers: she weighs them like goods. She tells them to keep a hand off the table.

Sample: "A man with no water is a body, and a body is trouble. Leave him his water and he's a fool who walks on. Fools talk less than bodies."

### The Army wagon driver on the radio

Vocabulary: daylight, hollows, tracks, tires, dust and rain. He compares things to the weather on the road.

Rhythm: fast and plain, in whole sentences that he cuts off when he must drive. He uses radio words like "over and out".

Habits: he says he is short of daylight. He answers only the question asked, then adds one road warning.

Cares about: reaching a place before dark. He avoids anything that sounds like trouble he went looking for.

Strangers: he gives what he saw and asks for nothing back.

Sample: "Not that I saw. There were tracks all over the hollow, the wide kind raiders make with chains on their tires. They were old, though. Rain's come through since."
