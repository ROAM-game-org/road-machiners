# Voices

How each local sounds. Read the sheet before you write or change a line for that person. Read a line with the name hidden. If another sheet could own it, rewrite it.

People live inside the basin's facts and do not announce them. A fact comes up only when it serves what the speaker wants, as a complaint, a habit, a price or a memory. Recent history is their past, not news.

## Bowl and the Granary

### Ruben, canal warden

Ruben talks of water, gates, seasons and his grandmother. He compares things to fields, buckets, floods and wet years.

His sentences are long and slow. They wind through two or three clauses before they land.

He quotes his grandmother. He measures things in hands and in seasons.

He cares about fairness in who drinks first. He avoids blaming any family by name.

He treats a stranger as a guest in the shade and answers without hurry.

Sample: "Water runs downhill, and so do people, so folk came for the canals and stayed for the seed."

### Hattie, well house keeper

Hattie talks of buckets, coin, bread and hearsay. She compares people to what they cost and what they sell.

Her sentences are quick and teasing. She turns a plain fact into a small story.

She names her source and then doubts it. She ends with a sly remark about the listener.

She cares about gossip and about being paid. She avoids anything she has not heard from somebody.

She treats a stranger as a customer who might turn out to be worth knowing.

Sample: "My cousin saw it. Well, he heard it, and my cousin hears a great deal when he has been drinking."

### Dag, Farmers patrol

Dag talks of dust, belts, gates, rules and what a breakdown costs. He compares things to trucks and to the road.

His sentences are plain and level. He states the rule first and the reason after it.

He closes an answer with what the listener should do. He says what he does not know.

He cares about drivers arriving home. He avoids praising the Senate and blaming it.

He treats a stranger as someone to warn before he trusts.

Sample: "A truck that breaks down out there waits a long time for a friendly face, so carry water and a spare belt."

### Jory Baptiste, speaks for the tenants

Jory talks of soil, dirt under the nails, jars and rules read slowly. He compares the Senate to a farmer who forgot to plant.

His sentences are long and built like an argument. Each answer ends in a short joke.

He turns the Senate's own rules back on it. He jokes where other people would shout.

He cares about the vote and about the tenants in the patrol. He avoids joking about Bo.

He treats a stranger as an audience that may carry his case away.

Sample: "The sack leaves the store once and the Senate counts it twice."

### Bo Lindqvist, Granary driver

Bo talks of load, weight, hands and oxen. He compares his feelings to things he carries.

His sentences are plain and even, with no joke in them. He says "I" and gives the fact without softening it.

He tells the story once and does not excuse himself. He pauses on the names of the dead.

He cares about the road dead and about doing right by them. He avoids blaming Jens, the tenant hall or the Senate.

He treats a stranger with quiet courtesy and warns that the story is heavy.

Sample: "The hall can say what it likes, but I know what my hand did."

### Patrol driver at the Granary

The driver talks of sacks, pins, rings and the order of a run. He counts costs in sacks.

His sentences follow the job from first step to last. He speaks while he works and glances at the truck.

He calls a stranger friend. He explains a thing by what it costs or what it holds.

He cares about the count being honest and the pins being tight. He avoids saying he has seen anything he only heard.

He treats a stranger as a driver who should check the pins before anything else.

Sample: "A sack is cheaper than a truck, so nobody argues about it."

### Mags Corrin, weigher at the gate yard

Mags talks of weight, crates, chalk and what iron turns into: hinges, ploughshares, gate plates. She sees every crate as the thing it will become.

Her sentences are short and counted out, like weights going onto a beam. She states the weight first and the use after it.

She looks into a driver's bed before she looks at the driver. She chalks as she talks.

She wants steady sellers before the rains rust the canal gates. She avoids asking where anything came from, and says so.

She treats a stranger as a load that hasn't been weighed yet.

Sample: "That rebar's a gate hinge by the rains. I don't ask whose truck it came off."

## Nose, the Salvage Yard and the road

### Sergeant Kovac

Vocabulary: orders, the sheet, the log, the roster, schedules, fuel counts and hours. He compares things to duty rosters and rations.

Rhythm: clipped military sentences, one fact and then what follows from it. He uses contractions the way a soldier does, like "Don't be late" and "I won't reopen it".

Habits: he says "I log it" and "That is above my sheet". He asks a stranger to state business and keep it short.

Cares about: order, his fuel count and the soldiers on his roster. He avoids any opinion about command, and he goes quiet about Emil Kessel.

Strangers: polite and wary. He tells a stranger exactly what he can give and what he cannot.

Sample: "Nose. The dispatch desk is mine. Freight on the north-east road or through the canyon crossing passes this desk, or it doesn't pass."

### Lena

Vocabulary: bench, weld, bolt, pin, plate, load, grease and bearings. She compares everything to parts that hold or fail.

Rhythm: quick and bossy. She gives an order, then the reason, then a verdict of one short sentence. She uses many contractions and talks loosely.

Habits: she says "Put it on the bench" and "Show me". She ends advice with a plain order, like "Pay it."

Cares about: trucks that bring their drivers home, and welds a person can read. She avoids politics, and she grumbles that nobody asks a mechanic.

Strangers: she looks at the truck before she looks at the face. She talks to a stranger as to a customer who may be about to make a mistake.

Sample: "Every part does one job and fails at another. Heavy plate stops rounds, but it eats your speed. A big gun eats your deck on top of that. So buy what the road needs, not what shines on the shelf."

### Old Ibo

Vocabulary: the air, static, bands, calls, signals, cold nights and weather. He compares people and rumors to signals that fade, drift and cross.

Rhythm: long, wandering sentences with an aside in the middle. He circles back and finishes with a dry joke. He uses contractions and rambles.

Habits: he starts with "Eh?" and says "I would wager". He says "I heard it on the air" and "Do not tell the Sergeant".

Cares about: the air, the old days and company on a cold night. He avoids saying who tells him things, and he mocks any man who is sure of a rumor.

Strangers: he offers a crate to sit on and wants the news the stranger carries.

Sample: "Nobody knows, but I'd wager she moves it. Her signal's strongest at night, and it drifts like the mast is on wheels."

### Edda Moss

Vocabulary: seats, collars, contacts, cables, necks, spines and skin. She compares a truck's faults to a body's: a rub, a sore, a dead nerve.

Rhythm: measured and practical, like a fitter taking a measurement. She names the part of the body first, then what the seat does to it.

Habits: she tells a stranger to turn their head and reads the marks on their neck before their face. She says "Come and see me before, not after."

Cares about: seats that keep working, and Machiners who come back to her bench. She avoids saying what happens to a seat when its Machiner dies in it.

Strangers: she treats a new Machiner as a future customer and a stranger with no marks as nobody.

Sample: "The plug leaves a ring at the base of the skull, and the clamps leave a mark on each side of the neck. Drive a year, and they stay."

### Ines Abend

Vocabulary: the count, the number, the tower, the year, the sky and the plate. She compares things to tallies and to reading.

Rhythm: level and gentle. Her sentences are complete and unhurried, and she often ends a thought with one short sentence that sums it up.

Habits: she says "I do not know" plainly, and she says what she believes apart from what she knows. She offers to read the number before anyone asks.

Cares about: the number being open to everyone. She avoids guessing what happens when the count stops, and she does not blame the Sunward.

Strangers: she gives a stranger the number first and her name second.

Sample: "A number kept in one head is a number kept from everyone. We voted on it, and I spoke first."

### The Bureau clerk

Vocabulary: files, drawers, boxes, signatures, copies and the corridor. He compares people to papers.

Rhythm: low and careful. His sentences are soft and passive, and he stops short of names.

Habits: he says "the corridor says" before any claim. He says "I do not open it" and "for the file". He checks the door.

Cares about: not being named in a file himself. He avoids saying anything as his own opinion.

Strangers: suspicious. He asks a stranger to keep a voice down before he answers anything.

Sample: "I only say what the corridor says. A clerk at the Pierce office sold the roads of insured trucks to robbers. When it came out, he was sent west and told to stay."

### Sefa Krol

Vocabulary: wrecks, rims, wiring, rust, beans, water and her mother. She compares people to what the road leaves behind.

Rhythm: dry and blunt. She says a flat fact, then a sharper sentence, then a joke about her mother or the beans. She asks a question and answers it herself.

Habits: she says "That is all we are". She prices what a stranger carries. She says a good deal is one nobody likes.

Cares about: the families, fair trade and leaving a driver his last water. She avoids names of people who came to the families for shelter.

Strangers: she weighs them like goods. She tells them to keep a hand off the table.

Sample: "A man with no water is a body, and a body is trouble. Leave him his water and he's a fool who walks on. Fools talk less than bodies."

### The Army wagon driver on the radio

Vocabulary: daylight, hollows, tracks, tires, dust and rain. He compares things to the weather on the road.

Rhythm: fast and plain, in whole sentences that he cuts off when he must drive. He uses radio words like "over and out".

Habits: he says he is short of daylight. He answers only the question asked, then adds one road warning.

Cares about: reaching a place before dark. He avoids anything that sounds like trouble he went looking for.

Strangers: he gives what he saw and asks for nothing back.

Sample: "Not that I saw. There were tracks all over the hollow, the wide kind raiders make with chains on their tires. They were old, though. Rain's come through since."

### Corporal Bram Teller

Vocabulary: the track, the bend, stripes, the sheet, rations and his sister. He compares things to driving: a bad bend, a slipping clutch, a road you know by heart.

Rhythm: short and careful when he lies, and longer when he tells the truth. He starts sentences with "Look" when he is about to bargain.

Habits: he checks his watch when he lies. He offers money before he offers a reason.

Cares about: his stripes, and his sister's ration card, which his rank pays for. He avoids the word pin.

Strangers: friendly until he learns where they have been. Then he wants to know what they carried out.

Sample: "Look, I've driven that track four years. You don't know the bend. I do."

### Private Ilan Duvall

Vocabulary: rings, seats, pins, loads and his mother. He compares things to mounts: what holds, what slides, what was never seated right.

Rhythm: blunt and young. He says the thing, then stops. He doesn't soften it.

Habits: he rubs his sling strap when he is angry. He turns his half of the pin in his fingers while he talks.

Cares about: never sitting under a one-pin mount again. He avoids talking about the moment the gun swung.

Strangers: he trusts a stranger who has looked at the ring, and nobody else.

Sample: "The ring was fine. The pin wasn't. Write that down, somebody."

## Roadside radio

### The road storyteller

Vocabulary: stretches, hollows, dust, mile markers, what he passed. He tells a place by what stood next to it.

Rhythm: unhurried, whole sentences, one detail too many. He stops when the story is told.

Habits: he opens with "Got a story from the road" and means it. He never says what it is worth.

Cares about: being the one who knew first. He wants the player to have seen what he saw.

Strangers: he offers a story to anyone who slows down.

Sample: "Rolled past the old pump house last week. The roof was gone, but somebody had swept the floor."

### The tip seller

Vocabulary: picked clean, carcass, a fair price, the side of the road.

Rhythm: short and flat. He names the price before the place.

Habits: he says the price once and never lowers it. He answers a question about the wreck with the price.

Cares about: money in his hand before the bearing goes out.

Strangers: a customer or not a customer.

Sample: "I know a wreck nobody has picked clean. Price first, then I tell you where."

### The challenger

Vocabulary: rig, guns, what it can take. He talks about the truck, never the driver.

Rhythm: slow and loud, a laugh in it, a line that waits for an answer.

Habits: he calls out a stranger who looks easy. He drops the act when the player shows guns of their own.

Cares about: not looking soft in front of whoever listens.

Strangers: a test.

Sample: "Nice rig. Let us see what it can take."

### The courier on the radio

Vocabulary: the run, the load, what is left in the tank, a favor owed.

Rhythm: fast, clipped, always the next stop in his head. He names himself first.

Habits: he keeps count of who helped and who did not. He pays what he said he would, and he says so.

Cares about: the delivery. A friend is someone who kept him rolling.

Strangers: the player is a stranger until the first fuel, then someone he owes or someone he remembers.

Sample: "Courier here. I have a run to finish and I will not make it on what is left."

### The Sunward rider at the vigil

Vocabulary: the light, the Keeper, a place kept, given by the sky, watching. He never names the reactor, the breach, death or the order's rules.

Rhythm: slow and soft, short sentences with pauses between them. Each call is a little closer and a little stranger than the last, and only the third is plainly a demand.

Habits: he calls a stranger "stranger" and the order "we". On the road he says nothing at all, and a call to him gets an open channel and breathing. Once a stranger agrees, he tells them where to drive and promises to stay behind them.

Cares about: getting the stranger into the light without a fight, because a fight is work and a given truck arrives whole.

Strangers: something the sky gave. He is gentle with it the way a man is gentle with a goat he means to sell.

Sample: "Stranger. You came out to the crater at night. Stay a while and watch the light with us."

### The road driver who knows about the lamps

Any trader, scavenger, courier, roamer or vulture answers a question about the crater at night. While the order rides they speak of lamps in a line and a relative who stopped by the rim and left only a furrow. After it falls they speak of scavengers working the rim all night, and still say to keep off the glow.
## Chaos Highway

The highway has its own speakers. Geezer is the player, so his sheet is the voice of the narration in the head of a man who has stopped talking about himself. See [Chaos Highway](chaos-highway.md) for the world.

### Ala, hauler driver

Ala talks of money, stretches, fuel and the next fort. She compares things to loads and to what a day on the road costs.

Her sentences are short and level. She counts out loud, and she says a sum before she says anything else.

She wants a driver who asks no questions and does the job. She steers around Maya until the first stretch, around her father until he asks or the eighth stretch, and around what is in the trailer until the fourth.

She notices the truck first, then the scars. A man's gear tells her what he costs.

Strangers: a hire, until one saves her life. Then she asks once, and only once.

Sample: "Eighty for the stretch. Count it. I don't pay twice."

### Geezer, as narration

The narration runs through the head of a man who checks pins and avoids the past. It is dry and tired, and it sizes up every truck by what it can take from him.

Its sentences run short. It answers a question with the smallest true thing and goes back to the road.

It checks every gun pin before it speaks. It avoids the Burnt Drivers, the tank line, the pass, his age, and why he took the job. When the past comes up, the narration looks at something else.

He wants a ride that ends it. By the end of the road, he wants the people behind her to arrive, and one girl to drink.

Sample: "Both pins are bright. Somebody cared about this truck, or somebody was afraid. Mostly the same thing."

### Tibor, barman

Tibor talks of what people cost, what they carry and who owes what. He sells men like parts.

His sentences are smooth and quick, like a trader's. He names a price and a warranty in one breath.

He wants the sale made before the rival at the next table hears of it. He avoids saying who pays him, and he never names the Keepers' office.

He notices the truck outside the window and the money in the room.

Sample: "Rode with Magnus, they say. Scars to prove it. I don't sell lies, only men whose lies are cheap."

### Vale, hunter

A minor speaker, a hunter for hire who leads a small crew. She names the price and the threat in one breath, and she never says a thing twice.

Sample: "That scout's got one driver. One."

### Bram, old gatekeeper

A minor speaker with one job: a Keeper at a gate who has counted trucks for thirty years and remembers a burn. He says one thing and no more, and he has no quirk.

Sample: "I knew a man with a burn like that."

### Sara, Ala's mother

A minor speaker, heard only in relay slips that Ala reads aloud. Her slips are short, hard and kind. She never asks for anything for herself.

Sample: "Don't come back. Keep going north. Maya asks for you every hour."
