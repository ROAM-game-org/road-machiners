# NPCs

NPC activities, traits, decisions, states, escorts, raider crews and hired guards. The principles behind them are in [DESIGN.md](../../DESIGN.md).

## NPC activities

NPC behavior has three layers. Traits are permanent and set the chances of choices. A goal stack keeps long-term work under interruptions like fights, flight, service and repairs. The driver usually resumes that work once an interruption ends. Decision points pick reactions by weighted chance when a new hostile, contact, attack, prey, stranded truck or passed wreck appears. Traits give fixed knowledge of towns, salvage sites and hunting grounds. Each NPC remembers the subjects it already decided on and the attackers still in sight. There is no live shared intelligence. Drivers also keep hidden memories of what they saw, each with when they saw it. A newer memory of the same thing replaces the old one, and a memory fades after a day. The first kind is shop prices: a driver remembers a shop's prices when it does business there, and tells of them on the radio. The second kind is salvage it found stripped. The third kind is a truck that attacked or robbed it, kept for six days, and only the courier story reads it. The hover panel shows what a seen driver is doing and why, under its name. It also shows every state the driver holds toward the player, like feuds, grudges, revenge and deals, with the turns left. The full log debug flag logs each goal change and its reason. Messages name an NPC by its profession and driver name, like Roamer Silas Kane.

A driver never stands still for good. Of two drivers closing on each other, one waits while the other goes around.

While the Sunward order stands, drivers keep away from the Fallen Sun at night: they drop work there, leave the crater by the nearest edge and route round it, and go back to it by day. The order's own cars keep their own schedule, as [World](world.md#the-sunward) describes. It waits for a parked truck ahead only while that truck is about to drive. A point it cannot reach, like one another truck covers, counts as reached once it gets as close as it can. A driver that still stays put for a while with its goal out of reach drives nose first to a random free spot nearby and tries again. It backs there only if the spot lies almost straight behind.

NPCs spawn with a chassis from their role's table, then an optional cargo part, then gear, then goods. Every truck spawns at least twice as fast as a crawl. The gear is chosen from every part in the game. Each gear level gives a driver gear money to spend past its chassis, separate from the driver's wallet, and the role's four priorities decide what that money buys. A driver picks one part at a time. It looks at three random offers and takes the best gain for its money. One pick in ten is a random offer instead. The score has three parts: armor, guns and speed. The role's armor and firepower priorities weigh the first two, and its speed priority weighs the speed the truck keeps for the fuel its engine burns, since the driver pays for fuel from its wallet. So a fast, thirsty engine like the turbine is rare, and most drivers take a frugal diesel or a racing V6. Chaos Highway enemies and the Chaos Highway runner bot weigh speed alone. Armor counts each side by its quality, and thin armor adds little. Guns count each side by the arc and facing of its guns, and a side gains less from each gun after the first few. A driver mounts a gun only where its own tall parts leave it at least half its arc. Raiders, lawmen, Marshalls, vultures, convoy guards, mercs and Sunward cars always buy a real gun, and their cargo part and utility never take its room. Outriders and gunwagons keep room for a haul, 200 kg and 3 cells and 400 kg and 5 cells, so a robbery can fill their hold: gear that would eat the room is not mounted. Raiders roll the standard level or better. Every part an NPC spawns with rolls its wear from the level's table. Three parts in four are one step from junk and none are better than two steps worn, so loot sells near scrap and good gear is bought. A poor truck is one step worse again. The same world seed and actions produce the same equipment. There is no separate loot roll on defeat.

- Scavengers collect salvage and sell cargo. A scavenger on a trip stops for three in four wrecks it passes, then mostly goes back to its trip.
- Vultures prowl lonely roads and hunting grounds, and stop for nearly every wreck, pile or knocked-out truck they pass. They sell the loot, with armor and cargo packs on their trucks.
- Traders buy profitable cargo, keep money for upkeep and flee from threats. A trader rolls its run from every shop pair. It sizes each run's load to what it can afford and fit, and drops a run whose load profit does not pay the fuel for the trip from where it stands to the source and on to the buyer. It weights the rest by that profit above the fuel over the trip length. Near runs win most rolls, so traders spread over all shops instead of all taking the one best run. Traders start with 4000 money as working capital and spend at most 2500 on one load, so a loaded trader is worth robbing and one robbery does not ruin it. Up to eight traders drive at once, so a robber who patrols between the towns meets one every few hours.
- Raiders hunt the hunting grounds nearer their own camp, never ones a Bowl Farmer or Nose Army patrol could see from its farthest stop. A raid ends at a hide within a short drive of its ground, off the road and, where hills or props allow, out of sight of road traffic, where the raider waits parked for 40 turns. A parked truck makes no sound and no dust, so passing traffic notices it only within sight. A watching raider hears prey from farther than other drivers react, and mostly springs on it: it drives to where it hears the prey, following the sound as it moves, and judges the prey once it sees it. A raider on the hunt weighs a load by a lower cargo curve than one off duty, and it rarely flees from prey it can manage, so a lightly loaded player is attacked about half the time and a rich load nearly always. An outmatched or weak raider still flees. When a hunting raider sees the player and lets them pass, its hover says why: nothing worth taking, busy with another fight, too tough to take on, a load not worth the risk, or just let you be this time. Between raids raiders patrol around their camp from post to post. On a raid, a patrol or an investigation a raider drives over open ground and only crosses roads, so road traffic does not see it coming, though its dust shows from far. A raider that retreats, flees or is stranded keeps off the roads the same way. They fight, collect wreck cargo, and sell it at their camps and the Salvage Yard. A raider that comes away from a fight with cargo nearly always drops its hunt and takes the cargo home to sell. They buy fuel, supplies and repairs at their nearest camp, and flee to a camp or a town. A raider is fit to hunt while it can drive, has a working gun, and both its truck and its driver are above half. An unfit raider heads for its camp, even with no money, and takes no raid or patrol. If camp service cannot make it fit, as with an empty wallet, junk guns or a hurt driver, it lies up at the camp until it is fit or its camp's refill time has passed, then gets fresh gear. A camp is a fence: it buys any good and spare part at the road price, half the value, keeps no stock and sells the cargo before it repairs. The Salvage Yard pays more but buys only scrap, parts and tools, so a raider with other goods goes on to a camp. Hunting grounds are lonely road stretches far from any site, and the ground inside each territory.
- Each NPC pays for fuel, supplies and repairs from its own wallet. Traders and supply convoys start with 4000 and every other driver with 1000.
- A service buys supplies before fuel, so a driver short of money still eats.
- A service repairs the cores, engine and weapons with all the money left, and every other part only with the money above the upkeep reserve.
- Duty pay covers one leg of work: the plain-engine fuel for the leg's tiles, the supplies for its turns and the lowest wage for its turns. A thirsty engine still costs its driver the difference.
- An NPC heads for fuel when its tank drops below a reserve for the way to its nearest pump. A pump is a town or a stall that sells fuel. A broke driver measures to a town, or its camp for a raider, never a stall, since only those serve it. It judges that way as a straight line at the heat where it stands. Each driver misjudges by its own fixed share, traders keep a smaller reserve and cowards a larger one. A winding road, a hotter noon or a fight on the way can drain the tank before the pump, so some drivers run dry and wait for a tow. A driver also fills its tank whenever it sells or buys cargo at one of its pumps. Standing at one of its pumps, it also fills there when its tank holds less than that reserve for the leg ahead: the straight way to the leg's end and on to the pump nearest that end. It does so only if the pump has fuel and it can pay. A follower judges by its leader's leg. A driver offers a tow only when its tank holds that reserve for the way to the stranded truck, on to the tow site and on to its own nearest pump.

A driver knows a truck it saw and now only hears as the same truck. It decides on a hostile once, whether it saw or heard it first, and a truck heard first gets a second look when it comes in sight. A driver remembers a truck it ran from for about an hour and a half after it last saw or heard it. In that time it does not drive to look at its engine, and it runs again when the truck comes in sight. A fleeing driver keeps its flee point while the point still leads at least 45 degrees away from the threat, so a threat that circles it does not turn it back and forth. Idle drivers mostly fight manageable hostiles and flee stronger ones. A healthy driver busy with work, or on its way to a deal, mostly keeps on when a hostile appears that is not aimed at it or a nearby ally. A shot at a driver or a nearby ally, hit or miss, prompts a decision to fight back, flee or rarely keep on. Damaged NPCs react to visible hostiles before starting repairs. A driver counts as weak when its cab or its truck as a whole is badly broken, or when it cannot drive. One broken wheel is not enough. A weak driver mostly flees or begs. A driver whose truck can only crawl does not run, go looking for a sound or set out after a hostile it sees. It crawls on toward its stop, fights back when shot and begs. If it cannot get away from an active fight, it turns on the foe it still sees instead of standing still on a blocked trip for repairs. A driver judges force by the target's nearby visible group against its own nearby visible group.

A driver with no room for any good, by cells or by mass, takes no detour to salvage and rolls no scavenge or haul. It sells its cargo instead. A driver learns what a wreck holds only when it reaches it. When nothing there fits but a sale would make room, its hold counts as full: it drops its scavenge and loot goals, takes no detour to salvage and rolls no scavenge or prowl until its hold frees cells, as a sale does. When even a sale would not make room, it passes up that wreck for as long as the wreck lasts. A driver that reaches a wreck, pile or site and finds it empty remembers that for a day, and passes it up while it remembers.

A started refit on a knocked-out truck finishes before a repair or service trip takes the driver off the loot goal. Only urgent supplies interrupt it.

A driver that makes no headway in one spot, even by driving back and forth against a wall, gives up its goal and takes a new one after a hundred turns.

An NPC stays parked on the pad of a town, stall, camp or oasis for a few turns for each resupply, sale, purchase or load, with a progress bar over its truck, and the deal happens when the bar fills. Driving off or combat cancels it, and it starts over.

NPCs carry and use utilities by the player's rules. At spawn each truck may roll one utility for its type. Scavengers, roamers and convoys favor the patcher crane, and scavengers and vultures the scraper's knife. Lawmen carry flare cannons and harpoons, and a harpoon always rides beside a real gun, and convoy guards flare cannons and smoke mortars. Only a heavy or loaded merc rolls an emitter. Each turn a driver picks its utility orders:

- Sprout: an attacker is in sight, and the driver flees a fight or its cab is below half.
- Caltrops and Oil spiller: the driver flees with a hostile in sight behind it within 10 tiles.
- Smoke mortar: a fleeing driver shells the midpoint between itself and its nearest attacker in sight.
- Flare cannon: at night, a driver investigating a contact lights the contact.
- Emitter: in combat, every truck it sees in range is hostile, and there is at least one.
- Claymore ram: the driver has chosen to ram. It disarms an armed ram once it is out of a fight and has chosen no ram.

The harpoon rolls with the utilities, but it is a gun, so a driver fires it at its fight target like its other guns.

Scavenging is a timed search. The truck parks at a stock and searches for turns in proportion to what still lies hidden there, with a progress bar. Each turn turns up some of the hidden loot, see [Economy](economy.md). A finished search opens the stock beside the truck's grid, and the player drags in what they want. What they leave stays at the site for later. NPC scavengers take the revealed loot that fits.

Territory loot spots and road wrecks hold finite stock rolled at world creation and hidden until searched: goods, parts and sometimes a spare mountable part. The whole map holds loot worth well under the upgrade ladder. Each day a loot spot regains a small share of a fresh roll as hidden loot, up to its table's highs, so an emptied spot takes about two weeks to fill. Destroyed NPCs leave a wreck with the same kind of stock, shaped as their own burnt chassis. One truck at a time searches a wreck or a pile, and any other truck waits or leaves. A driver that reaches loot another truck at peace is looting argues once while that truck stays in sight: it warns the looter off about two times in five, leaves about half the time, and fights over it about one time in thirty. Against a looter it judges a threat it mostly leaves. Raiders, scumbags and brave drivers warn and fight more, traders, cowards and couriers leave more. A warned driver complies and leaves, refuses, or fights back. After a refusal the warner leaves about three times in four and fights otherwise. Drivers never argue with a hostile truck or a deal partner, and they pass up loot such a truck holds. Their mounted parts join it at the HP they had, and their built-in parts turn into parts good worth a share of the chassis value. A looted road wreck goes after about a day, and a new road wreck appears elsewhere on a road. Both happen beyond the player's gray vision, so the road wreck count stays the same.

A cargo handed to an NPC robber is only what the robber can load, on its own pile. Dumped items and cargo spilled from a broken cargo part go on a ground pile. Drops close together join one pile. A pile disappears when empty or after two days. Any collector can take from it. A pile handed to an NPC is that NPC's claim until it has taken it, gives up or 60 turns pass. The hand-over is a truce that ends the fight between the two sides at once, so the robber goes straight for the pile. The nearest robber in the fight that sees cargo spill from its target claims it too, and its claim holds while it fights. An NPC target answers the claim as it answers a threat: complying leaves the pile to the robber for a truce and keeps the rest of the cargo. The claimant warns others off, and they answer as any warned looter does. An NPC that backs off leaves it alone. A refusal, a fight back, or a take in the claimant's sight starts a fight.

Shops have unlimited money. Goods prices move with trade, and part stock is finite. Initial NPC resources, including the working capital of traders and convoys, are explicit sources. So are patrol pay, which a town gives its lawmen at each patrol stop, and dispatch pay, which a shop gives a courier that arrives at it. Fresh gear at the end of a lie-up is one too, at most one loadout per driver per refill time of its type. There are no offscreen catch-up grants.

## NPC traits and states

Every NPC carries a set of traits instead of one class. Each trait adds activities and shifts chances. A scavenger with the scumbag trait still scavenges, and it also robs. Traits roll at spawn: every scavenger scavenges, and some are also scumbags or cowards. One neutral driver in four is a scumbag and one in four a coward, whatever its kind. One neutral driver or raider in seven is brave, unless it is a coward. Lawmen and convoy guards are always brave. Lawmen roll no other traits. Traits stay hidden, so the player learns a driver is a scumbag only when it starts acting like one. The Perception perk Read the driver shows traits in the hover panel.

- Scavenger collects salvage and helps stranded trucks.
- Trader buys and sells between towns, rarely starts a fight, and sometimes fights back.
- Raider watches the roads from posts near the hunting grounds of its own camp, patrols between posts around that camp, investigates distant engines, keeps off the road while it hunts, takes robbed cargo home to sell and knows the raider camps.
- Scumbag robs trucks that carry a valuable load and look weaker than it.
- Coward flees more often and fights back less.
- Brave almost never runs, pleads or gives up its cargo.
- Lawman patrols the roads out to about a sixth of the way to the other town and fights Scrapjaw and Kiln raiders on sight. Its home town pays it at each patrol stop. It attacks whoever fires the first shot at a lawful NPC it sees or starts robbing one, the player included. [Factions and relations](factions.md) has the rule.
- Vulture prowls the hunting roads for wrecks and knocked-out trucks, and rarely helps anyone. Most vultures are cowards and some are scumbags.
- Courier travels between towns and locations, and almost never stops for salvage. Couriers run in pairs of two couriers, and one follows the other as its escort. The town or stall a pair reaches pays each of them for the trip, and a territory pays nothing.
- Roamer mostly explores random places, off road too, and trades and scavenges when it finds a chance.
- Supplier hauls fuel drums from the Pump Station to the towns. About one trip in three it buys goods for up to 2500 and trades them between towns instead.
- Guard follows a supply convoy and protects it.
- Merc waits at a town for hire, then escorts its client, or rides guard for the player for a day.

A chance is 0 only when an option is physically impossible or a trait forbids it. A driver with no working gun cannot fight, and a truck with no loot cannot be robbed. Supply convoys, convoy guards and lawmen never rob. A driver following a leader never robs, but its leader can. A stranded driver cannot rob: it starts no robbery, and a robber stranded mid-robbery gives it up and backs off its prey, unless the prey is fighting it. A stranded driver still fights back when attacked. A cheap load is robbed only rarely: cargo value counts goods and spare parts, not mounted gear, and a robber weighs it only once it has looked the truck over. Anything a driver can do keeps at least a 1% chance. So an ordinary scavenger robs about once in a hundred chances, and a trader sometimes starts a fight.

Drivers judge each other by the odds of a fight between them, worked out with the real damage rules. Each working gun stops a truck at its shots per turn over the rounds that truck's side takes before its cab, engine or transmission breaks, so armor counts on the side it covers and a gun counts on the sides its arc reaches. A fight is fought from one side of each truck, and the faster truck picks the pair of sides that suits it, so a fast truck gets behind a truck that only shoots forward. Danger is how many times stronger the other side looks: the driver's odds to lose over its odds to win. A tank looks more dangerous than a scout, and a beaten tank less. Danger counts a group on each side: the truck, the trucks it rides with as escort, crew or hired guard, its nearby allies, and any truck fighting one of its foes that is hostile to none of them. Only trucks the judging driver sees count, and each counts once, so a convoy guard is not counted twice and a crew mate behind a hill adds nothing. A driver deciding whether to take on a truck also counts every other hostile truck it sees, so a weak truck beside a strong one is not a weak target. The progression bots decide fights by the same rule. A truck that can never stop the driver is no danger to it, even when the driver cannot stop it either. A driver misjudges another truck's danger by up to a quarter each time it first sees it. A driver takes on a truck whose danger looks no more than its boldness, and runs from one that looks stronger. It runs only when it can get away: it must outrun the threat, and its rear must outlast the threat's fire while it drives out of range. A driver that cannot get away mostly stays to fight back or gives in, since running would only show the threat its rear. A runner keeps going until it has not seen, heard or been hit by anything hostile for 6 turns, so a long gun out of sight does not turn it back into range, and it stops looking for the truck it ran from. Each turn a fight hurts it, the driver judges the danger again with the damage taken, and it may break off a fight that turns bad. A scumbag robs a truck whose danger looks below its boldness. Scumbags and brave drivers are bold, and cowards are timid. A stronger target, or one near a town gate, is robbed only rarely. In a fight, a driver steers for spots where its rounds are likely to land and get past the armor on the side the target shows, and the target's are not. Hit chance falls with distance, so it closes in when its guns gain more up close than the target's, stands off when they gain less, and works round to a bare side. A driver gives up a fight that has not worn its target down by a twentieth of its condition in 20 turns. It backs off that target, as a failed robber does.

A robber knows a truck's goods and spare parts only after it looks the truck over, by the player's Look-over rule. Before that it judges a load by what shows: the room in the truck's hold, at a fraction of the mean crate value. A robber that likes the look drives to within 10 tiles, holds its fire and spends a turn looking the truck over, with a progress bar over its truck. Then it robs what it saw, or backs off a load not worth the risk. A raider sizes up its prey the same way, unless the prey attacked it first. A robber that loses its prey from sight and hearing for 6 turns backs off. A robber never demands cargo or a cash cut from a truck it has not looked over.

A driver looked over by a truck it sees rolls once while that truck stays in sight: it keeps on, keeps away, or, against the player, radios a warning. Traders and cowards keep away most, and brave drivers, raiders and scumbags warn most. A look-over is not an attack and starts no feud or combat. When a driver looks the player over in the player's sight, the log says so.

A robbery is an attack. Most robbers radio for the cargo first, the player and NPCs alike. NPC prey answers at once: it hands the cargo over and makes peace, fights or runs, and outgunned prey mostly pays. A robber asks once, so after a refusal the fight goes on. The winner loots the knocked-out truck or the wreck the loser left. A raider, or a driver that robs, that helps knock out any other NPC driver goes to strip its truck too, unless it is hurt or stranded. A robber whose target escapes backs off that target for a while.

In a fight, each chance to ram the target is a decision. The driver weighs the ram against its guns by expected damage. The ram counts the damage it forecasts on the target minus the damage it costs the driver, weighted by part: the cab, wheels, engine and guns count most, and armor and ram bars least. It also counts the chance the ram connects, which falls with distance and with a target that moves across the line. A ram that costs the driver more than it deals is rare, and traders almost never ram. A fighter with a ram bar picks fight spots that line its nose up at the target. Trucks of similar weight ram when the forecast pays.

A shot at another vehicle is an attack, hit or miss. The victim starts a feud with the attacker, and so do the witnesses that step in by the rules in [Factions and relations](factions.md). A damaging crash between hostile trucks is an attack too.

A damaging crash between trucks at peace is most likely an accident. Each damaged NPC decides once whether to forgive it or retaliate. Most drivers forgive. Raiders and scumbags retaliate more often, and a crash with an ally is nearly always forgiven. A retaliating driver starts a feud as if it was attacked. Contact with the truck on a tow rope counts for nothing.

A driver hurt by a hostile may plead with it. It asks for a truce, or it begs for mercy when it is weak. Traders and cowards plead most, and raiders seldom. The other side decides whether to accept. A robber or raider after the other truck's cargo seldom pleads. If the truck it robs carries cargo and the robber is not weak or outgunned, it names its price instead of granting a truce, and an NPC prey answers it like any cargo demand: it gives the cargo up, fights or runs. Prey without cargo can still roll a truce with it, rarely. It never robs a truck it holds a truce with. A truce ends the feuds between both whole sides: each truck, the trucks riding with it in its sight, its nearby allies and the trucks in sight fighting beside it. A plea to a crew member is answered by its crew leader when the leader is awake and in sight. The player never answers for a hired guard. Mercy is a truce the beggar pays for with its cargo, which it drops for the winner to take. A driver rarely pleads with the same foe again soon after a refusal. A granted plea leaves no such wait, so a driver whose truce breaks can plead again at once.

A driver keeps its word. A driver retreating after a defeat keeps it too. A trade meeting, a patch, a tow, a tow on its way and an escort are deals. The two sides of a deal never rob each other. The driver that made the deal starts nothing of its own until the deal ends: it robs no one, tows no one else, loots nothing and hires no one. A driver holding a pile claim starts nothing new either until its claim ends. It still reacts to hostiles, contacts and attacks, but a deal counts as work: a healthy driver bound for a deal mostly keeps on against a hostile that is not aimed at it or a nearby ally, and it is not keen to start a fight with one it sees. A driver in combat calls off its patch, trade and aid deals and drops a tow. A deal kept to its end leaves both drivers backed off from each other for a while. A driver attacked by its deal partner fights back or flees as usual, and it also wants revenge on that truck.

States are timed relations between two trucks. Each ends as expired, fulfilled or broken, and each ending can start other states.

- A feud makes both trucks hostile. Sight or shots between them keep it going. It expires after some turns without either, and a failed robber then backs off.
- A tow runs from the offer to arrival in town, where the fee is paid. It breaks for free when the player refuses, unhitches or drives away from an offer, or when the tower meets danger or the trucks turn hostile.
- A tower the player turned down rarely offers again.
- A tower with a client on the rope ignores a hostile it only hears. A hostile in sight or a shot at it still makes it drop the tow.
- A tower that dropped a tow for danger comes back with the same deal, until the truck drives again. It waits ten turns beside the truck before it hitches or offers again, so a hostile at the edge of sight does not make it hitch and drop on every turn.
- A driver with no working engine is serviced in a town, never at a stall, since only a town gives it a fresh loadout.
- Only one driver answers a stranded player at a time. Near a town gate, fewer drivers offer a tow.

## Escorts

One truck can follow another as its escort. The follower rides beside its leader's tail at the leader's pace, and resumes following after any fight or tow. The leader waits for an escort that falls a little behind while following, but not for one coming back from afar, and not while it heads for its own service stop. It does not wait for an escort busy with a fight or a service stop, which catches up after, nor for one that is stranded or on a tow rope. A courier leader waits for its partner up to 150 tiles back, and a partner that flees runs to its leader when the leader lies away from the threat. An escort far behind drives at full speed and keeps its aim until the leader has moved a good way, so a long chase does not rebuild its route every turn. It treats shots at the leader as shots at itself, so it fights back or flees as it would for itself. It tows a stranded leader to the usual tow site.

A trader that has just bought cargo to carry to another shop may instead post an escort job on that shop's board, more often on a dangerous road. It then parks off the pads and waits up to 75 turns for a taker. A free merc it sees can still hire it, which takes the offer down. A trader escorted by the player drives its trip, brakes while the player is behind it and drives on while the player is ahead. It waits for a stranded or towed player wherever it is, up to the 120 turns of its patience, and it waits where it is under the radio order Wait here until the order Let's move or 90 turns pass.

A trader, courier or roamer on its way to a site may hire a free merc it sees. The fee is the merc's plain running cost to the destination and back plus the lowest wage, and the client must afford it above its upkeep reserve. The merc accepts or refuses, and a hurt merc refuses more often. The client pays once, when it reaches the destination. A territory counts as reached at its edge, where a trip there ends. An escort ends unpaid when either truck is gone, knocked out or turns hostile.

Each supply convoy spawns with a guard that escorts it. The convoy pays its guard each time it loads, buys or sells at a site, up to the money it holds. A threatened or warned-off driver whose escort is in sight and awake gives way a tenth as often, whoever makes the threat, so a demand on a guarded convoy or a trader with a hired merc mostly starts a fight with the escort. A guard whose convoy is gone joins another unguarded convoy, or drives between the towns until it finds one.

A courier pair is two couriers. When one is knocked out or wrecked, the escort ends, and the other is a lone courier. A lone courier mostly joins the nearest lone courier of its faction that is not hostile to it, and a knocked-out courier does the same when it wakes. When a courier is lone and the cap has room, the town sends a new courier from the nearest site to ride with it, and with no courier at all it sends a pair. Chaos Highway has no pairs. Generic pairs are not Tamsin Roe's pair, who stay a story.

## Raider crews

A raider at its own camp, fit to hunt and riding with nobody, may gather a crew when it sees another free raider of its camp there. Scrapjaw raiders only ask Scrapjaw raiders, and Kiln raiders Kiln raiders. It drives up to each one in turn and asks once. The other raider joins or declines. A brave raider joins more often and a coward less, one that rates the asker stronger than itself twice as often, and one carrying loot to sell half as often. A raider that the asker attacked or robbed, or that holds a feud, grievance or revenge against it, never joins. The log tells each answer and why, and when the crew rides out or nobody joins. The gathering waits at the camp for raiders coming in, up to 40 turns, and stops when the crew is three trucks. With at least one member the leader goes on a raid and the crew follows it. A raider that gathered a crew, or failed to, does not try again for 150 turns.

A crew hunts as a unit for 300 turns and then splits up, and the members go back to their own lives. Members ride beside the leader and stop beside it at its watch post. When the leader fights a truck a member sees, the member chooses once to join, hold back or run, by its own judgment of the fight. A member that runs leaves the crew. Members rarely start a fight of their own while the leader is near, unless the hostile attacks one of them. They defend the leader like any escort. A member knocked out leaves the crew. When the leader is knocked out or wrecked, the member with the most firepower for its condition takes over and the others ride with it for the rest of the hunt. A crew left with one truck splits up.

## Hired guards

The player can hire a free merc in sight by radio with Ride guard for me for a day, while the two are at peace and the merc is calm. The merc takes or declines as it would for an NPC client, and a hurt merc declines more. A merc that declined is not asked again. Its fee is a day of the lowest wage plus its own supplies and fuel for a day's driving, about 80 to 115 M by chassis, paid at once and never refunded. The deal is offered only to a player who can pay. A merc can be hired while the player is in combat, and a merc has no help topic.
