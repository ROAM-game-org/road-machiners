# Writing dialogue

This guide covers how people in the basin talk and how the player talks back. It holds for town locals, radio calls and the spoken lines inside page scenes. [Writing quests](quests.md) covers ink, quest structure and page narration. [Voices](../lore/voices.md) keeps one sheet per speaker. [Character lines](character-lines.md) says how each spoken line is generated from that sheet.

The examples are written for ROAM. A line marked Bad is the kind of line that has shipped before and confused players.

## House style

ROAM's voice comes from the author's own wasteland and adventure stories. These habits hold for page narration and for talk alike.

### A voice, not a camera

Narration runs through the character's head. Between the plain facts, the character thinks: a judgment, a complaint, a question to themselves, a guess that may be wrong. "Bradley was a good axe, even though he was an insufferable dickhead." "Should have approached from the sun side." "Does dying by a bullet beat starving in the desert? Hell yeah." In a page scene this is the second person: "Walking south-west was stupid. Not that the south-east had anything better."

A page of bare facts, one after another, reads as a telegram even when each sentence is fine. "The house has lost its roof. The fence posts lean. The tap gives nothing." lists what a camera sees. Give the facts a reaction, or join them to what the character wants.

About a quarter of the sentences carry this voice, as in the author's stories. The rest say plainly what happens. The habits below are the strongest kind of voice, so save them for a turn in the scene, like the moment before a fight. A simile or a joke on every line flattens all of them, and so does none at all.

Describe what matters to the character right now, like water, shade, a threat, a way out, or a sight that stops them. A place may be beautiful or grim when the character notices it. Never pad a scene with scenery.

### No line only the writer understands

Every line must make sense to a player who has read nothing but the screens before it. A clue works when the player sees the thing happen, not when the writer knows what it means. "Every knot is dry" means nothing on the page. Lowering the rope and pulling it up with no wet knot shows an empty well.

Use common words. Write "doorway", not "doorsill", and "the gun mount", not "the swivel ring", unless a person in the scene would use the trade word and the scene shows what it is.

### Read people by their gear

Bring a person in with a short list of what they wear and carry, then the narrator's practical read of it. Boots, tools, wear and dirt say what a life has been. A narrated list may run in fragments. Speech still uses whole sentences.

"A Kiln rider. Goggles pushed up, a scarf gone stiff with dust, a rifle with a new stock on an old action. The boots are new. Somebody paid him lately."

### The narrator sizes things up

Narration stays close to the player and thinks like a survivor: what's a threat, what's worth taking, who has water and who's lying about it. Its judgments are short and dry. It gives the practical read of a moment, never the moral one.

"Somebody loved this truck, or was very afraid. Out here that's mostly the same thing."

### Small stakes beside big ones

Put a plain want next to the violence: a bed, a hot meal, a cup of water, who does the washing up. A haggle over a cot after a gunfight tells the player more about the basin than any speech about how hard life is. End a big scene on the small thing.

After the player drives the raiders off the pod field, Juno says: "You'll want the cot by the stove, then. And you're fetching the water in the morning."

### Both sides look the same

When two groups face off, let the narration notice how alike they are: the same rags, the same thirst, the same bad odds. The basin has no clean side, and the narration shows it by putting the two sides next to each other, not by saying so.

"A pair of trucks on patched tires faces a pair of trucks on patched tires, and everyone squints into the same sun."

### Customs nobody explains

Show a town by its habits: where guns hang, who says their name first, how long you wait before you speak, what you leave on a grave. People keep a custom without thinking and get uneasy when a stranger doesn't. The player learns it by watching someone keep it or by breaking it.

### Old words on new things

The old world left words behind, and people use them for new things without knowing the old meaning. Use a few and never explain where they came from.

"A bath at Green Pit is a wet rag and a cup of warm water, and it costs extra."

### Talk is a trade

Gatekeepers, traders and clerks price news. A toll drops if you tell the gate what you saw on the road. A stranger's story is worth something to somebody, and people say so.

Kovac: "Tell me what's parked at Kiln, and I'll tell you which road is clear."

### Swearing like weather

Rough people swear plainly and without heat, the way they'd mention the wind. Swearing belongs to some speakers and not to others. It's never there to shock.

### Competence shows in small checks

Show skill as habit. A veteran checks the tires and belts at every stop, counts their knives before a talk and notices the man who steps out of their line of sight. The narrator respects it in a few words.

"The old one takes a casual step to the side, out of your sight. Figures how he lived this long."

### Hope, then the floor

Give the player a reason to hope, then take it away with a plain fact they see for themselves. The rifle across the hill was a plank. The water barrels were full of sand. The reversal comes from what the character notices, never from a narrator's twist.

### Violence is fast, then ugly

In a fight the narration narrows to a few physical details and short sentences. After it, the character is cold and shaking, and the narration finds no glory and no comfort. Killing is work you get through. It never feels good afterward.

"Then it's over. Your hands won't stop shaking, though you're cold all over in the heat. The other driver's canteen is still half full. It's the first thing you think about, and you don't like yourself for it."

### The old world is a dream

Memories of before come vague and too bright: fountains, grass, lights at night. The dust is what's real. People don't mourn the past out loud. It comes up, and they shrug it off.

### Refrains and endings

A scene may carry one phrase that comes back changed at the end, like "Sand and dust, the spotlight of the sun." Use one per scene at most.

End a scene on a dry, small line, often the next chore or a flat joke. Never end on a moral.

## Before you write a person

Write their voice sheet in [Voices](../lore/voices.md) first. A sheet answers five questions.

1. What one speech rule shows in every line they say? Kovac states one fact, then what follows from it. Hattie asks where you came from, then tells you the plain truth. Ruben winds through water and fields before he lands.
2. What do they want from the player? A stranger is a buyer, a pair of hands, a witness, a messenger or a risk. A person with nothing to want has nothing to say.
3. What do they notice first about the player? It is the most visible thing: a battered truck, a Nose seal on the door, a stranger towed in on a chain, a name on someone's list.
4. How long do their sentences run? A boaster runs long. A veteran runs short. A frightened clerk starts sentences and abandons them.
5. What do they avoid talking about? The thing a person steers around tells the player more than the thing they say.

Match the voice to the place. Bowl talks of fields, seasons and family. Nose talks of orders, rations and the log. The Yard talks of prices and what things are worth. A stranger learns the town from how its people speak before anyone explains it.

A minor speaker gets a clear job and no quirk. A gate sentry with a funny tic reads as someone important, and the player wastes time on him.

### One question, three voices

The player asks "Is the north road safe?"

Kovac: "No. Kiln riders work it past the second ridge. Go in daylight, and log out with me first, so somebody knows where to look for you."

Hattie: "Safe enough till the culvert, then I hear there's shooting, so turn that fine truck round if you hear it too."

Ruben: "Safe is a word for a canal with good walls, and the north road has no walls at all. Go early, while the dust is still lying down. You'll see anyone coming from a long way off."

Each answer tells the player the road is risky and what to do about it. Hide the names and you still know who spoke.

## The first line

The opening line gives the speaker's name, what they do and what they offer, in one breath. Then it reacts to the player if there is something to react to.

Bad: "The wind talks, if you know how to listen."

Good: "Lena. I fix the Army's trucks, and anybody else's if they pay. If it's broken, put it on the bench."

Good, after the first fall: "You're the one they dragged in on a chain. Sit down before you fall down. I'm Hattie, and this is my cistern."

Vary the greeting on what changed since the last visit. Kovac greets a player who caught the depot thief differently from a stranger. A person who sees the player for the third time with the same question says so.

## Answers

### Answer first

The first sentence answers the question that was asked. Attitude, memory and color come after.

Asked "Where can I buy fuel?":

Bad: "Fuel is a matter of who you know, and who knows you."

Good, Sefa: "At the pump by the Yard gate. Ours is cheaper, but it's mixed with whatever was in the drum before."

Asked "Why pay Kiln at all?":

Good, Odile: "Because if we don't, Kiln takes the grain trucks on the road, and a Granary that loses its trucks loses its winter. My grandmother lived through the year the grain ran out. She ate boiled sacking. I'd rather pay raiders every season than see that again."

### Facts come through a life

A world fact comes up as part of what the speaker wants: a complaint, a price, a habit, a debt, a memory. The player learns the fact and why it matters in the same line.

Bad: "Pierce Corporation insures trucks and lends money to the towns."

Good, a Granary driver: "Pierce wouldn't pay when my brother didn't come back. They're the insurers at Nose. They take your M's every season, and when he went missing they found a reason: he had pulled his gun off the bed to save weight."

People live inside the basin's facts. Guns on every truck, the Kiln fee and the Senate are old and ordinary to them. Nobody announces them.

Bad: "Every truck carries a gun now."

Good: "Check your brake rod before the bend. A cheap one snaps, and then nothing slows you."

### Explain local words in the same answer

The player is a stranger. When a speaker uses a local word, the same answer says what it is, in the speaker's own way. Each answer makes sense to someone who heard nothing before it.

Bad: "Some. Good drivers, bad paper. They wear the same coat."

Good, Dag: "A few, yes. They drive well, but no tenant can pay for Pierce paper. So they ride in our coats, and the Senate keeps their names off the roll that would earn them a vote."

### Depth on the player's terms

Give the short answer first. Let the player ask for more with a follow-up choice. A follow-up opens only after the answer that mentions its subject.

Ruben, asked "Who runs Bowl?": "The canal families, through the Senate. It's slow, and nobody I know has starved waiting for it."

Then the choice "[What's the Senate?]" opens, and the longer answer lives there.

A talk window shows only the last reply, so the reply that opened a follow-up may be gone when the choice appears. Name the subject in the option itself, with a noun the player has met, and never with "it", "them" or a quote of the last reply. The speaker never says "you can ask me about that". What they say or do gives the reason to ask.

Bad, Sefa: "I make a point of never taking the last of it from anyone, and you can ask me why." Then "[Why do you never take the last water?]", which shows up two picks later with nothing before it.

Good, Sefa: "My families pick stalled and knocked-out trucks clean, down to the frame, and the driver keeps his water." Then "[Why leave a stripped driver his water?]".

### Important facts live in more than one mouth

A fact the player needs to move on, such as where South Lock is or who buys stolen fuel, comes from at least two people or from a person and a document. A player who skipped one talk is never stuck.

## Questions the player asks

### A reason to ask this person

The player only asks about what their character knows: something the speaker said earlier in this talk, a note they hold, a place they have found, or something the narration has shown them, like a bandaged arm or the gun on a truck. Gate the choice on the variable that the earlier answer sets, on `has_note()` or on `found()`.

A question also needs a reason to ask this person in particular. It follows from what the player just saw or heard from them.

Bad, asked of a stranger at a gate: "Were you in the Old Army?"

Good, after the driver said his uncle starved at the Old Orchard gate: "What happened at Old Orchard?"

### Options are short and do one thing

An option states one intent in a few words, usually under eight. The answer carries the voice, not the option.

Bad: "[I was wondering whether you might tell me something about the raiders and also the road.]"

Good: "[Who are the raiders?]"

A hub shows two to four questions plus a way out. More than that means the talk has no subject. Give each talk one subject and let it end.

### A spread of tones

When the moment calls for a reaction, offer real attitudes, including a cold one and a foolish one. The player builds who they are by choosing.

After Bo says he shot Jens Vosk on the road:

- "[He'd have shot you first.]"
- "[You killed a man over grain.]"
- "[Say nothing.]"

Each choice changes what Bo says next. None of them is a test with a right answer.

### Exits and actions

The way out is always there. It can be plain, like "[Goodbye.]", or in the moment, like "[Leave him to his tires.]". An action the player does instead of saying uses a bracketed verb, like "[Put the M's on the table.]".

## Dodges, lies and refusals

A dodge must look like a dodge. The speaker hedges, changes the subject or answers a different question, and the player gets a clear choice to press.

```ink
+ [Who brings you the fuel?]
  People with cans. Next? # speaker: Sabine
  ~ sabine_dodged = true
  -> hub
+ {sabine_dodged} [A corporal brings it, doesn't he?]
  She stops wiping the board. "Big hands. A scar on his chin. He never gives a name, and I never ask." # speaker: Sabine
  -> hub
```

A person who refuses gives a reason tied to the player. The refusal tells the player how the town sees them.

Good: "I don't sell to anyone on Kovac's sheet. Get your name off it and come back."

A speaker who talks in riddles says plainly that they won't answer, and still gives one plain fact before the talk ends. A riddle with no exit is a wall.

## Skill checks in talk

Tag every choice that can fail with `# check: <skill> <level>`, and branch on `passed()` after it. The window shows the skill, the level and the player's own level, so the player knows the risk before the pick. Narrate the success or the failure plainly, and never repeat the level in the text.

A failure changes how people treat the player. It costs money, a door, a name on a list, or trust. It never only closes the choice.

Good, a failed machining check at the Yard: the player calls a weld bad, and Lena says, "My brother laid that weld. Find another bench."

An unlocked option can still be a bad idea. Knowing more than the speaker can make them close up. A player who reads the ledger aloud to the clerk who wrote it learns the clerk's name and loses his help.

Never chain a hidden second check behind a passed one. If a talk needs two skills, show both choices at once.

A failed check still gives a full scene. Write every failure as carefully as the success, because many players see it first.

## What people want

Every speaker who matters wants something from the player and goes after it in the talk. They raise their own topic, steer back to it and offer a trade.

The reason a person gives is often not the real one. Odile asks the player to find who takes the sacks. What she wants is a small man she can put out of the gate, so the tenants see something done.

Warm voices carry plans too. A friendly steward who pays well for a lost bundle is still buying the player's silence.

## Remembering the player

Decide what each speaker would notice first, and react to that. Keep the callback small and specific. One line that remembers what the player did is worth more than a branch nobody sees.

Good, Kovac after the player named Vance: "Sergeant Kovac, dispatch. The depot's been quiet since you came through. Don't make me miss you."

Repeat visits can escalate. A person asked the same favor three times grows short, and says so in character.

Cover the first town fully and the far places lightly. A reaction most players never see is a line nobody reads.

## Humor

Humor comes from the speaker's own logic. The speaker does not know they are funny.

Good, Jory: "Either the pipe exists or those cabbages are saints."

Say a grim fact flat and let the player react. The flatness is what lands.

Good, Sefa: "What we find of their dead, we bury with their boots on. Nobody on either side likes it, which is how you know it works."

No jokes that need knowledge from outside the basin. No jokes about the game itself, its skills, its menus or its numbers.

## Sentences

- Use contractions. How often a person contracts is part of their voice. "I do not" and "it is" in every line make everyone sound like the same stiff translator.
- Write whole sentences with the rhythm of speech. Do not stack clipped fragments as shorthand.
- Terse is a voice, not a license to be cryptic. A short-spoken Nose sergeant still says complete things a stranger understands.
- Do not chain clauses with "and" and "which" to fit in explanation. Split the run-on and cut what the answer does not need.
- Keep a reply under about forty words. A longer answer splits into two lines, each with one idea.
- No semicolons in any quest text, in English or Russian. Say it as two sentences. English quest text also holds no em dash, en dash or spaced hyphen used as a dash, and none of the stock phrases of `src/test/prose-tells.ts`, like "a testament to" or "a sense of". Russian keeps «—». The voice test fails on any of them.
- Never bold speech. A person stresses a word through how the sentence is built.
- Stay in character. People never talk about game systems: turns, quests, XP, levels, HP, the map or the player. The words keep their everyday meaning, so "the road turns" and "water level" are fine. Ordinary numbers are fine too, like "two soldiers". The voice test in `src/test/out-of-character.ts` checks the game phrases.

## Before you commit

1. Hide every name and read the talk. If a line could belong to anyone else, rewrite it.
2. Read it as a stranger who has heard nothing. Every word they meet is either explained or plain.
3. Read it aloud. A line you stumble on is a line the player skips.
4. Play every branch with `npm run quest -- <quest>`, including the failures.
5. Read the changed lines against the [Humanizer](humanizer.md#how-roam-uses-it) the way its How ROAM uses it section says for fiction.
6. Put every practice, cause and detail the talk states through the [sense check](../lore/README.md#sense-check).
